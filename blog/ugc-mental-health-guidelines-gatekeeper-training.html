The UGC draft names gatekeeper training. Here is what it asks colleges to do

A Supreme Court judgment in July 2025 put a uniform mental health policy for Indian higher education on a legal footing. The draft guidelines that followed name gatekeeper training as something institutions should provide.

2 September 2026 · 6 minute read · by Niranjan Ramamurthy

In July 2025 the Supreme Court ruled in Sukdeb Saha vs State of Andhra Pradesh and directed that India needed a uniform mental health policy for its higher education institutions. A committee was convened under the University Grants Commission, chaired by Rajinder K. Dhamija of the Institute of Human Behaviour and Allied Sciences in Delhi. Its draft, the UGC Guidelines on Uniform Policy on Mental Health and Well-Being for Higher Educational Institutions, went out for public consultation in January 2026.

For anyone running a college, the draft is worth reading properly rather than in summary, because it moves several things from good practice into expected practice.

What the draft asks for

Every institution is to have a Mental Health and Well-Being Centre and a monitoring committee, staffed by qualified professionals. The draft sets a staffing figure, which is the part most institutions will feel first: one mental health professional for every hundred students. Reporting of the numbers to UGC becomes routine rather than occasional.

Institutions are also asked to display Tele-MANAS and the UGC anti-ragging helpline through campus signage and digital channels, and to collaborate with the National and District Mental Health Programmes for outpatient care and referral, rather than treating the campus as a closed system.

Where gatekeeper training appears

The draft does not leave the training question vague. Among the activities it sets out for the Mental Health and Well-Being Centre is providing training, and it names gatekeeper training as an example, directed at faculty.

That is a regulator naming a category of training rather than a supplier, which is the right way round. But it matters commercially and practically, because it converts a discretionary spend into something an institution can be asked about.

The reasoning behind it is not complicated. Teaching and non-teaching staff are asked in the same document to watch for social withdrawal, absenteeism, agitation and increased risk-taking. Asking staff to notice those things without telling them what to do next is an incomplete instruction. Gatekeeper training is the part that closes it.

The number behind the policy

The National Crime Records Bureau recorded 13,892 student suicides in India in 2023. That was the highest figure in a decade and roughly 8.1 per cent of all recorded suicides that year.

Two cautions about that number, both of which apply to any NCRB figure. It counts recorded cases, and under-reporting in suicide statistics is well documented, so the real figure is higher by an unknown margin. And a single year is a data point, not a trend. What supports the trend reading is the decade of figures behind it, not the 2023 number alone.

What the draft does not settle

It is a draft. Consultation closed on 29 January 2026 and the final text may differ, particularly on the staffing ratio, where reported figures have varied between one professional per hundred students and a split arrangement of one counsellor per five hundred students with peer supporters at one per hundred.

It also does not say who delivers training, to what standard, or how often it must be refreshed. Those are decisions institutions will have to make on their own, and the honest answer is that gatekeeper training decays. A member of staff trained once in 2024 and never since is a weaker link than the training record suggests.

If you are working out where to start

The order that has worked on the campuses we have trained is narrower than the draft implies. Wardens, counsellors and student peer mentors first, because they are who students actually approach. Then teaching staff. Then everyone else, if there is budget.

Institutions above a few hundred people usually find it cheaper to certify a small number of their own instructors than to buy seats indefinitely. That is the model Andhra Pradesh used across its government medical colleges, and the arithmetic is straightforward once the cohort passes a few hundred people.

Sources: Sukdeb Saha vs State of Andhra Pradesh (Supreme Court of India, 25 July 2025); UGC draft Guidelines on Uniform Policy on Mental Health and Well-Being for Higher Educational Institutions, released for consultation January 2026; National Crime Records Bureau, Accidental Deaths and Suicides in India 2023.
